# Recent increase in the number of Super Typhoons in the Philippines

Joseph Basconcillo<sup>\*</sup>, Noel Bangquiao

Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration, Department of Science and Technology, Quezon City, Philippines

Available online 11 September 2025

## Abstract

The negative impacts of tropical cyclones (TCs) on the Philippines' socioeconomic development continue to rise, despite previous reports indicating a decline in the annual number of TCs within the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR). Over the past 44 years, an average of 170.000 individuals have been affected annually, with total TC-associated cost damages reaching US\$3.5 million annually. Here we provide evidence that the increasing TC-associated cost of damages can be linked to a significant rise ( p < 0.01) in the frequency of Super Typhoons (STYs) in the PAR since 1998. Notably, from 2003 to 2020 (Period 3, x = 3.05), the annual STY frequency increased by 106 % ( p < 0.01) relative to 1993 to 2002 (Period 2, x = 1.48). Such an abrupt shift in STY frequency coincided with the transition of the Atlantic Multidecada Oscillation into its positive phase in the mid-1990s, which possibly strengthened the Pacific Walker Circulation. This transition also may have contributed to conditions that favored the development of more intense TCs, including warmer sea surface temperatures in the Western North Pacific, a westward expansion of the Western North Pacific subtropical high, a weakened East Asian subtropical jet stream, and a favorable environmental steering flow—all of which contributed to increased TC activity within the PAR. These findings aim to improve the understanding of the abrupt changes in TC activity in the Philippines, assisting regions in anticipating evolving TC-associated risks, which are expected to enable better disaster preparedness, infrastructure planning, and early warning systems. Additionally, they provide additional insights that can guide disaster risk reduction and preparedness strategies, facilitating and enhancing long-term TC prediction.

## 1. Introduction

Tropical cyclones (TC) are one of the most destructive hydrometeorological hazards to which the Philippines is vulnerable, along with heavy rainfall, droughts, and floods, which are responsible for loss of lives and damage to the country’s economy (Espada, 2018; Basconcillo and Moon, 2023). It is reported that the annual frequency of TCs monitored in the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) has been decreasing from 1980 to 2011 (Cinco et al, 2016; Corporal-Lodangco and Leslie, 2016). We corroborate such findings by showing and extending the period of analysis of TCs from

1979 to 2022 and show that both the annual TC frequency and landfalling TC frequency in the PAR have a declining trend (Fig. 1a).

The Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration (PAGASA) classifies TCs in the PAR in five categories (PAGASA, 2024) based on associated maximum wind speed: tropical depression (<62 kph), tropical storm (62–88 kph), severe tropical storm (89–117 kph), typhoon (118–184 kph) and super typhoon (>185 kph). This classification was adopted in 2022, during which PAGASA updated its TC categories. To check whether such reclassification inflated the annual STY count, we plotted the annual number of TCs with maximum wind speed greater than 185 kph and the annual number of typhoons using the old (118–220 kph) and updated (118–184 kph) definition (Fig. S1). Expectedly, the number of TCs based on maximum wind speed does not differ from the number of STYs. The number of TYs using the old definition exhibits a similar increased pattern since the early 2000s, while it is not apparent in the updated TY classification, indicating that the updated TC classification system did not inflate but rather emphasized the recent increase in the number of STYs in the Philippines.

Although the long-term trend of TCs in each category varies, Super Typhoons (STY) exhibit a consistent declining trend from 1979 to 2022 (Fig. 1b–f). Similarly, landfalling STYs have also declined.

Although the annual TC frequency in the Philippines appears to have decreased in recent decades, its impacts, indi cated by TC-associated costs of damages and loss of lives, have become increasingly devastating. More specifically, the affected population and the cost of damages to agriculture and infrastructure have increased since 1979 (Fig. 2a and b). While all regions of the country experience varying levels of impact, more densely populated areas like Metro Manila and neighboring provinces (i.e., Pampanga, Cavite, Bulacan) registered higher number of affected population (Fig. 3a). Meanwhile, the northern parts of the Philippines (i.e., Apayao, Kalinga)

and some regions in the eastern Philippines sustained the highest TC-associated cost of damages (Fig. 3b). Although TCs contribute to these rising costs, other factors such as increasing population density, expanding infrastructure, rapid urbanization, and differences in policy measures also play a significant role. These factors increased exposure and vulnerability over time, resulting in higher reported costs from natural hazards, even if the physical intensity of the hazards remains the same. To account for inflation, all damage costs were adjusted using the 2018 Consumer Price Index (CPI) from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas.

The apparent contrast between the declining annual frequency of TCs in the PAR and the increasing TC-associated impacts in the Philippines highlights the need for further investigation. Although the overall trend appears to show an insignificant decline, the time series of annual STY frequency reveals an abrupt shift in trend, showing a positive recovery since 1998 (Figs. 1f and 2c). The pre-1998 period (denoted by PAR STY 1 in Fig. 2c) exhibits a significantly decreasing trend $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), while the post-1998 period (denoted by PAR STY 2 in Fig. 2c) shows a significantly increasing trend $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Given these trends and the increasing impacts of TCs in the Philippines, we aim to provide insights into the reasons behind the recent increase in more intense TCs, particularly STYs, in the PAR. It is reported that there was a recent increase in the Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE) or TC activity in the PAR since 2003 (Basconcillo et al., 2022). The increase in TC activity in the Philippines is linked to the westward expansion of the Western North Pacific (WNP) subtropical high, which has steered more TC passages into the PAR and ultimately resulted in increased TC activity. The same study further emphasized the relationship between the ACE and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO). Since the mid-1990s, AMO transitioned into its positive phase, where warmer sea surface temperatures (SSTs) dominate the tropical Atlantic and the WNP, which shifts the convection patterns towards the WNP, resulting in favorable precursor conditions for increased TC activity.

Meanwhile, the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), which has also been reported to influence TCs in the Philippines (Kubota and Chan, 2009), shifted to its negative phase in the mid-1990s. In a negative PDO phase, a widespread warmer sea surface temperature is found off the coast of Japan, which weakens the East Asian subtropical jet stream and shifts the westward position of the WNP Subtropical High (Basconcillo and Moon, 2022). These changes create a more favorable environment for TCs to enter the East Asian region through the northern Philippines, resulting in an overall increase in TC passages within the PAR (Basconcillo and Moon, 2022).

The Pacific Meridional Mode (PMM) is also known to influence TCs in the WNP more particularly during the peak TC years, such as the record-high frequency observed in 2018 (Gao et al., 2020; Basconcillo and Moon, 2023). Along with the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO) and the central Pacific Nino˜ (N4), the PMM plays a key role in modulating the location of TC formation in the southeastern quadrant of WNP. Their influence occurs mainly through warmer SST, reduced vertical wind shear, and increased relative vorticity—conditions that are favorable for enhanced TC formation (Basconcillo et al., 2021; Basconcillo and Moon, 2023; Zhao et al., 2018). Lastly, a cooler Indian Ocean Basin-wide SST is typically drawn to the warmer SSTs in the central Pacific. Such a temperature contrast leads to strengthened westerlies that converge with easterlies, increasing low-level water vapor flux and the continuity of cyclonic vorticity. As a result, deep tropical convection is further intensified (Basconcillo and Moon, 2023). As of this writing, we have not identified a study that specifically examines the relationship between the abrupt shift in STYs in the PAR with these climate modes. Therefore, our study is expected to provide new insights into the influence of the large-scale environment and the multiyear variability modes on the TC activity in the PAR, particularly STY. Additionally, such additional insights can facilitate and enhance long-range TC prediction activities aligned with the mandates of PAGASA, which are expected to enable better disaster preparedness, infrastructure planning, and early warning systems.

## 2. Data and methods

## 2.1. Data

## 2.1.1. TC dataset from PAGASA and IBTrACS

In 2022, PAGASA revised the classification of TCs in the PAR into five categories based on associated maximum sustained wind speed: tropical depression, tropical storm, severe tropical storm, typhoon, and STY. Previously, typhoon category was classified with a maximum sustained wind speed between 118 and 220 kph, while STYs had a maximum sustained wind speed exceeding 220 kph. Under the revised classification, typhoons are defined with associated maximum sustained winds from 118 to 184 kph, while STYs are associated with maximum sustained winds greater than 184 kph (PAGASA, 2024).

In our analysis, we reclassified the TCs based on their lifetime maximum intensity using the updated definition of TC categories, meaning that the old categories were replaced with the revised classification. With the revised classification system, the number of STYs is expected to be highlighted because stronger typhoons previously categorized simply as typhoons are reclassified as STYs. Our analysis considered only TCs that reached the STY category and intersected the PAR. The PAR is geographically bounded by several points defined as: 25<sup>◦</sup>N 120<sup>◦</sup>E, 25<sup>◦</sup>N 135<sup>◦</sup>E, 5<sup>◦</sup>N 135<sup>◦</sup>E, 5<sup>◦</sup>N 115<sup>◦</sup>E, 15<sup>◦</sup>N 115<sup>◦</sup>E, and 21<sup>◦</sup>N 120<sup>◦</sup>E (Fig. 4d–f).

Previous studies on TCs in the Philippines typically focus on the PAR as the primary domain of analysis (Basconcillo et al., 2022; Cinco et al., 2016; Corporal-Lodangco et al., 2016). The PAR, on average, is visited by 19–20 TCs annually (PAGASA, 2021). Here, we focus on TC frequency because it serves as a key indicator for detecting long-term trends and variability of TC activity (Sobel et al. 2021; Webster et al., 2005). The study covers the period from 1979 to 2022, ensuring the use of reliable tropical cyclone observations during the satellite era ( Knutson et al., 2019). For the TC best track data, we used the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS) version 4.1 because it provides homogenized data in the WNP region (Knapp et al., 2010). While there is an ongoing initiative to develop PAGASA’s Best Track dataset, its records currently until 2018; thus, we used the IBTrACS in this analysis.

## 2.1.2. Climate indices and reanalysis data

The climate indices used in our analysis are the AMO, PDO, PMM, IPO, IOBW, and the N4 . The AMO index is the 5-year smoothed area-weighted annual SST from 0 to 70<sup>◦</sup>N in the Atlantic Ocean; IPO) is the 5-year smoothed difference in annual SST anomalies over the central and equatorial Pacific and the northwest and southwest Pacific; PDO is the 5-year smoothed leading principal component of annual SST north of 20<sup>◦</sup>N in the Pacific Ocean; IOBW is the areal-averaged SO SST anomalies over the tropical Indian Ocean defined at −20<sup>◦</sup>S −20<sup>◦</sup>N, 40–100<sup>◦</sup>E; and N4 index is areal-averaged SO SST anomalies over the central equatorial Pacific Ocean defined at −5<sup>◦</sup>S −5<sup>◦</sup>N, 160–210<sup>◦</sup>E. These indices were obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Physical Sciences Laboratory, Climate Prediction Center, and Japan Meteorological Agency- Tokyo Climate Center. The fifthgeneration European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) atmospheric reanalysis (ERA5) reanalysis data products are used in the analysis of SST and zonal and meridional wind.

## 2.1.3. Population affected and cost of damage due to TC

Data on TC-associated losses and damages were sourced from the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Council (NDRRMC) of the Philippines. The NDRRMC defines the affected populations as the individuals and commu nities that experience the negative impacts of TCs, including injuries, loss of life, displacement, loss of their homes and properties, disruption of access to basic services, and economic losses or disruption (PreventionWeb, n.d.). The Consumer Price Index (CPI) and population size for each province were obtained from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) and the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) based on the 2020 Census, respectively. To adjust for inflation, the cost of dam ages was deflated using the 2018 Consumer Price Index from the BSP, which is the latest index as of writing.

## 2.2. Statistical tests

To minimize the influence of the interannual variability and highlight the trends and shifts in the time series, we employed a 1-3-4-3-1 filtering technique on all the time series (Emanuel, 2007). Applying such a filter minimizes short-duration peaks while emphasizing broader trends over multi-year timescales aligned with our objective. We divided the STY time series according to the observed abrupt changes in mean frequency: Period 1: 1979–1992. Period 2: 1993–2002. and Period 3: 2003–2022. To test the power of the difference between the three periods, we performed a power analysis of the sampled means using an alpha of 0.05. The power analysis calculates the likelihood that the test of difference in the means will detect a true difference when one exists, ensuring that the sample size and test design are sufficient to identify effective changes in STY frequency over time. We used Pearson’s correlation to measure the linear relationship in all the indicated correlations, and used a t-statistic with a two-tailed distribution at 95 % confidence level. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two continuous variables, with values ranging from −1 (indicating a perfect negative correlation) to +1 (indicating a perfect positive correlation); a value of 0 signifies no linear correlation. The significance of the trend in the time series was tested using the modified Mann-Kendall Test based on the Hamed and Rao Technique to address the influence of autocorrelation (Hamed and Rao, 1998). The Mann-Kendall Test is a nonparametric method used to detect monotonic trends in a time series. The modification by Hamed and Rao adjusts the variance of the test to account for autocorrelation, which can otherwise lead to an overestimation of trend significance in autocorrelated data. Finally, we performed the same series of statistical tests on the landfalling STYs.

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Seasonality and power analysis

In TC variability studies, emphasis is typically given to the peak TC season (e.g., June to November in the WNP), as including data from outside this period can dilute the signal of the peak TC activity. Relying on annual data may obscure important seasonal patterns and variability essential for understanding the conditions driving the peak TC activity (Guo et al., 2023; Ogata et al, 2021). Previous studies have also shown that 86–89 % of annual TCs in the WNP occur during the more active June–November season (JJASON, Basconcillo et al. 2024; Corporal-Lodangco and Lesli 2016). While existing reports indicate that TCs are generally active in PAR during the JJASON, our analysis shows that STYs in the PAR also exhibit high activity during this period. Given their strong correlation with annual STY counts $( r = 0 . 9 8 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , the succeeding discussion focuses on STYs in the PAR during the JJASON season.

Moreover, recent reports showed that the western and eastern regions of the WNP exhibit spatial and temporal asymmetry in their response to large-scale environments, which results in distinct seasonal patterns of TC variability within the basin (Basconcillo et al, 2024). Our findings support this by showing that while the annual STY passage frequency within the PAR is strongly correlated with JJASON STY passage, the correlation between September-October-October (SON) and JJASON STY passage is stronger $( r ~ = ~ 0 . 9 2$ $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ than JJA June-July-August (JJA) and JJASON $( r = 0 . 7 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). This suggests that STY frequency during SON contributed more to the overall significant correlation of JJASON with the annual STY activity (Fig. 4a–c). Due to the considerably smaller number of STYs in SON compared to the annual and JJASON, we opted to proceed with the analysis using the JJASON data. Note that the maximum number of STYs observed in JJASON and annually reaches more than 5, whereas up to 3 STYs are observed in SON.

Furthermore, the power analysis of the sample means of Period 2 and Period 3 confirms that they effectively yield significantly different mean values (Table 1). For STYs, the risk of getting a significant difference in the mean value of STY frequency between Period 2 and Period 3, while such probability is true, is 0.856 or 86 %, and while it is false is 0.144 or 14 % of the time (Type II Error). For landfalling STYs, the risk of committing a Type II Error while it is true is 0.64 or 64 %, and while it is false is 0.36 or 36 % of the time. This is further supported by an effect size of 2.23 for STYs in the PAR between P3 and P2. In the context of statistical mean value comparison, an effect size larger than 0.8 indicates a significant difference between the two means. This large effect size between Period 3 and Period 2 further confirms that the observed abrupt shifts in STY frequency are statistically significant and distinguishable from one another.

## 3.2. Recent changes in STY mean frequency

It is reported that the number of annual TCs forming in the WNP has been decreasing since 1979, marking the start of the satellite era (Corporal-Lodangco and Lesli, 2016). It is widely regarded that the satellite era marks the reliable period of TC data in the WNP and across many regions (Kang and Elsner, 2012). Here we show that while the long-term trend of STY frequency in the PAR is decreasing, there are significant abrupt changes in the mean frequency that can be observed from 1979 to 2022 (Fig. 4a–c). The annual STY frequency peaks during Periods 1 and 3, with a dip in between during Period 2. The same seesaw pattern is observed for JJASON and SON seasons, respectively (Fig. 4b and c). A comparison of mean STY values, including landfalling STYs, between Periods 3 and 2 across all seasons shows that the mean STY frequency in Period 3 is significantly and consistently higher than in Period 2 (p < 0.01). Given these findings, the subsequent analysis focuses on the changes between Periods 2 and 3.

## 3.3. Influence of the large-scale environment

TC activity in the WNP is mainly driven by natural modes of variability and external forcings on various spatiotemporal scales (Huang et al, 2024; Basconcillo et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024). We examined the influence of large-scale environmental conditions on the abrupt changes in STY activity in the PAR by plotting the global composite maps of SSTs, 200 hPa velocity potential, and outgoing longwave radiation in JJA-SON during Period 2 and Period 3 (Fig. 5a–d), respectively. SST represents oceanic conditions where its warmer surface acts as a thermodynamic heat source for TCs, the 200 hPa velocity potential shows deep tropospheric motion where more negative values indicate stronger upward vertical motion, the 200 hPa wind anomaly denotes upper-level divergence and convergence, and lastly, the negative outgoing longwave radiation signifies convective activity, which are commonly associated with weak vertical wind shear. The composite maps of 300 hPa horizontal wind speed and integrated 850-200 hPa environmental steering flow in the WNP were also plotted (Fig. 6a–f), respectively. The 300 hPa is used to detect fast horizontal wind speed indicative of the location of the sub tropical jet stream, while the integrated environmental steering flow indicates the direction and strength of the surrounding environment that influences the movement of the TCs.

During Period 3, the SSTs are notably warmer across many regions worldwide, especially between 120<sup>◦</sup>E and 180<sup>◦</sup>E in the tropical WNP (Fig. 5a). Meanwhile, a band of cooler SST anomalies appears in the central equatorial Pacific, which is conducive for convective activities. As a result, a low-level divergence develops and generates low-level easterlies from both sides - the Atlantic and the Indian Oceans, which are drawn toward the warmer tropical waters in the WNP. Such an atmospheric bridge effect in the three oceans has been described in previous reports (Walker and Bliss, 1932; Wang, 2019). These warmer SSTs enhance ocean heat fluxes that thermodynamically fuel TC intensification (Delfino et al., 2024). Additionally, a negative 200 hPa velocity potential is present in the WNP—indicative of deep tropospheric upward motion (Fig. 5c). Such a strong upward motion is favorable for increased convective activities (Fig. 5e), which are associated with weaker vertical wind shear, creating overall favorable conditions for sustained convective activity and more intense TCs.

In addition, the warmer SSTs in the north Pacific off the coast of Japan are also conducive for rising motion (Fig. 5a), which have stalled and weakened the East Asian subtropical jet stream as depicted by the northward retreat of strong westerlies in Period 3 (Fig. 6a–c) and the strong anomalous easterlies along 30<sup>◦</sup>N (Fig. 6c). This aligns with the findings of Yang et al. (2002) that a weak East Asian subtropical jet stream follows a small meridional gradient of the western Pacific SST associated with cooling in the tropics-subtropics and warming in the extratropics.

A weak East Asian subtropical jet stream prompts the anomalous Rossby wave energy at upper levels to propagate toward the equator in the midlatitudes and toward the poles in the subtropics (Wang et al. 2012). This propagation strengthens and expands the WNP subtropical high, allowing it to extend westward. Furthermore, the WNP subtropical high was observed to have expanded westward during Period 3 (Fig. 5a, Fig. 6d and e). It is reported that when the East Asian subtropical jet stream is weak, the WNP subtropical high is westward-displaced (Basconcillo & Moon, 2022). The expansion of the WNP subtropical high intensifies the westward intrusion of cyclonic vorticity, which strengthens the westward movement of the environmental steering flow, increasing the number of TC passages directed into the PAR (Fig. 6f).

## 3.4. Influence of the AMO on super Typhoon activity in the Philippines

Among all the climate indices, the AMO has exhibited a consistent transition pattern since the mid-1990s. While AMO and PDO are well correlated with each other, the PDO has not consistently shifted to its negative phase; hence, we proceed with the AMO in our analysis. The AMO is a long-term mode of climate pattern characterized by long-term fluctuations in SSTs in the tropical Atlantic, cycling between warm and cool phases over a period of 60–80 years. The AMO is known to affect climate patterns far beyond the Atlantic basin through atmospheric teleconnection that reaches the WNP. Basconcillo et al. (2022) demonstrated the significant influence of the AMO on the recent increase in TC activity or ACE in the PAR since 2002. Here we do not attempt to analyze and explain the long-term co-variability between the AMO and TC metrics in the PAR in recognition of several factors, including the limited availability of long-term TC data, uncertainties regarding the internal variability of the AMO, and the specific objectives of our report - to provide insights into the recent increase in the number of STYs in the Philippines.

During the positive AMO phase and similar to the observed changes during Period 3, the SSTs in the tropical Atlantic become warmer, while the reverse is true for its negative phase (Fig. 5b). Warmer SSTs enhance surface fluxes and promote rising air motion, which triggers convective activities. The upward motion from the increased convective activities in the Atlantic Ocean spreads outward and descends over the cooler SST anomalies in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific (Fig. 5d). This descent leads to a low-level divergence system, which helps generate low-level easterlies that move toward the warmer SSTs in the WNP. As a result, a positive AMO shifts anomalous tropical convective heat sources toward the WNP (Basconcillo et al, 2022).

Similar to the observed environmental changes in Period 3, the AMO also plays a significant role by influencing the convergence of anomalous westerlies and easterlies, as well as enhanced deep rising motion shown by negative 200 hPa velocity potential anomalies in the WNP (Fig. 5d), indicative of deep tropospheric rising motion. This strong upward motion becomes conducive for sustained convective activities (Fig. 5f), which is linked to a weak vertical wind shear, and more intense TCs.

Meanwhile, the westward expansion of the WNP subtropical high is also observed in a positive AMO phase. Since the AMO is closely linked to the PDO $( \mathrm { r } = - 0 . 5 4 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ , warm SSTs are also observed in the North Pacific. This ocean surface warming promotes deep tropospheric upward motion, particularly off the coast of Japan (Fig. 5b), which can slow the eastward progression of the East Asian subtropical jet stream and consequently contribute to the westward displacement of the WNP subtropical high (Basconcillo & Moon, 2022). Such a westward shift strengthens the westward movement of the environmental steering flow, leading to an increased number of TCs entering the PAR.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

Through a series of observational analyses, we suggest evidence that the recent increase in the number of STYs in the Philippines since 2003 may be linked to changes in the largescale environment and correlates with the recent transition of the AMO into its positive phase in the late 1990s. This tran sition has influenced atmospheric circulation patterns and oceanic conditions in the WNP, making them more favorable for intense TCs in the PAR.

The AMO’s teleconnection with the WNP influences changes in the large-scale environment, such as increased ocean warming, rising vertical motion, and sustained convective activities, which leads to the weakening of the East Asian subtropical jet stream, the westward displacement of the WNP subtropical high, which ultimately steers more intense TCs into the direction of the PAR.

Given the strong correlation between the AMO and STY frequency, it is essential to assess the possible role of anthropogenic climate change in modulating such a relationship. Recent studies indicate that anthropogenic climate change has influenced the changes in the periodicity of the AMO and its recent shift to a positive phase through attribution with increased greenhouse gas emissions (Mann et al., 2020). While it remains a question whether the AMO is still considered a naturally oscillating system, if such a positive trend continues, it could result in future changes in the TC activity in WNP, thereby increasing the risks and impacts associated with STYs affecting the Philippines. However, since the AMO is also correlated with the PDO, which remains to be considered as an internally driven system (Beaufort and Grelaud, 2017), further research is needed to determine whether these observed changes in STYs are internally driven or if they are being exacerbated by anthropogenic climate change. Additionally, understanding the impact of anthropogenic warming on other key climate oscillations, such as the PDO and IPO, could provide further insights into the drivers of STY activity in the WNP.

In conclusion, our study underscores the importance of large-scale climate variability in shaping TC trends and variability in the Philippines. As compound climate and weather extremes continue to pose significant risks, understanding climate dynamics in the context of TC research will be crucial for reducing and mitigating the impacts of TCs and improving disaster resilience in the WNP.
